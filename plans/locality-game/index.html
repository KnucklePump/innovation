Locality Game

A serverless, data-sovereign proximity game for events: attendees' phones privately match business intent, ping you when the right person is nearby, and guide you together with an icebreaker in hand — no server, no badges, only inside the event and only while the app is open. Built for Canadian regulated-sector events where 'nothing leaves the phone' is a requirement, and differentiated by private matchmaking and genuinely creative games. Sold to the organiser.

1Executive Summary

The conference event-app market is mature and saturated — not the open field it first looks like. Whova alone (launched 2015) has powered 50,000+ events across 100+ countries; the large majority of organisers now use an event app and ~61% of attendees adopt one. So this plan does not try to out-feature the incumbents. It wins where their greatest strength becomes a weakness.

That strength is data. Whova, Cvent, Bizzabo, Brella and Swapcard run on server-side matchmaking, lead retrieval and analytics — the engine that lets them sell exhibitor leads and engagement dashboards. But it is exactly that engine which disqualifies them from the events that most need help: Canadian government, healthcare and financial-services events, where Canadian data residency is a hard procurement requirement ('nothing leaves the country', and buyers 'cannot accept flexible data residency arrangements').

Locality Game is a serverless, peer-to-peer proximity game: attendees' phones privately match nearby people and — on a capable phone — draw a precise on-screen arrow guiding them together, with no server between attendees, only inside the event's geofence, and only while the app is open. The data never leaves the phone, which makes it the ultimate data-residency answer — and one the incumbents cannot match without dismantling the very business model that funds them. The plan attacks that data-sovereign regulated-sector niche first, then widens to general Canadian events on differentiators the incumbents are weak on: matchmaking done privately and in the room — you name a role, an intent or a target company, and when a mutual match is physically nearby the app pings you both and hands you an icebreaker, a warm consented lead engine rather than a pre-event list (their documented failure is attendees 'overwhelmed, not guided'); the death of the badge — the laminate few people read, replaced by an app that does the job it never could; and genuinely creative games — a game-design craft (spy, battleship, team missions, custom-branded) versus a scavenger-hunt feature bolted onto a SaaS. Rather than fight the all-in-one battle, it gets in the flow by embedding into existing event apps, not by rebuilding registration or badge hardware.

The economics are unusual and lean. The founders build the app in-house (iOS and Android from day one — the core runs on the universal BLE radio, so it works on every phone; the UWB arrow is a later premium enhancement), so the build is sweat equity, not a cash cost. Because the game is serverless, gross margin runs near ~95% (no per-attendee hosting). Total capital is only ~$430k across two small staged raises, gated on a Calgary proof-of-concept. Revenue comes three ways from the organiser: a per-event licence, an annual subscription for repeat buyers, and a sponsor Booth-Crawl. The model turns EBITDA-positive in Year 3 and reaches a ~48% EBITDA margin by Year 5, clearing every bankability test on the base case — positive NPV, debt-service coverage far above 1.25× from stabilisation (Year 4), balance sheet tying every year, cash positive throughout.

Read the honest caveats, though. First, the moat is the crux, not a footnote: the proximity mechanic is copyable, and this is a focused wedge, not a broad platform — the board scored defensibility low ('feature, not a company') and this plan is sized and staged to respect that, not to argue it away. Second, most of the modelled value is terminal (exit) value, and the headline IRR is inflated by the tiny cash outlay (the build is free) — treat it as an early-stage venture bet, not an exit-independent return. Third, the honest downside is a real loss — but because the growth raise is gated, it is a choice to stop at Calgary-POC scale (~$280k exposure), not a national failure. The whole plan rests on two things the cheap POC is built to test: will a room actually play, and does the price hold.

2The Product / Service

Locality Game is one proximity engine that powers a live-event game, licensed and branded by the event organiser. Every attendee's phone quietly finds the right other people nearby, prompts a real-world interaction, and — on capable phones — draws a precise on-screen "radar arrow" that guides them together. Three deliberate constraints are the product, not limitations: it runs peer-to-peer with no server between attendees (so the data never leaves the phone — the answer to Canadian data-residency rules), it works only while the app is open (a closed app provably isn't scanning), and it works only inside the event's geofence. It runs on every phone from day one — the core (detect, private match, chat) rides the universal Bluetooth Low Energy radio on both iOS and Android; the UWB precision arrow is a premium enhancement on capable devices, never a gate on the core game. The launch focus is the Conference edition for Canadian regulated-sector events; the same engine extends to a festival/social edition ('Spark') as a later, unmodelled option.

Value proposition

For the organiser (the buyer): event networking is broken — only ~15% rate it 'very effective' — because incumbents hand attendees a list of hundreds of profiles and leave them 'overwhelmed, not guided'. Locality Game instead does the matchmaking privately and in the room: you tell it what — or who — you're looking for (a role, an intent, a target company), and when a mutual match is physically nearby, it pings you both and hands you an icebreaker. It is, in effect, the death of the conference badge — the laminate few people read: the app does the job the badge fails at — telling you who's worth meeting, that they want to meet, and how to open the conversation. And for the events that need it most — Canadian government, health and finance — it is the only option that satisfies data residency by architecture: nothing is tracked, nothing leaves the phone, no US cloud. For the attendee: meet the right people (privately matched to your goals — including the specific companies you want to do business with, a built-in warm-lead engine), with the confidence they want to talk and an icebreaker to start — on any phone.

Key features

  • Runs on every phone from day one — the core game rides the universal BLE radio on iOS and Android; the UWB precision arrow is a premium enhancement, never a gate on the core
  • Serverless peer-to-peer / data-sovereign — matches, profiles and chats never leave the two phones; nothing touches a server or a US cloud, the answer to Canadian data-residency rules
  • Private business-intent matchmaking — set a role, an intent or target companies; matched only when it's mutual (PSI), and nobody (not even a listener nearby) learns your criteria unless there's a match — matchmaking done in the room, not on a server
  • Warm-lead pings — when a mutual match, including someone from a company you named, is physically near, the app pings you both and hands you an icebreaker — a consent-based, in-person lead, not a scanned badge
  • The death of the badge — no laminate to print, wear or squint at; the app does the job badges fail at (who's worth meeting, that they want to, and how to open the conversation)
  • Guided, not listed — instead of a list of hundreds of profiles, a precise distance-and-direction arrow to the person worth meeting now
  • In the flow, no hardware — embeds into the existing event app via SDK, or offers a lightweight software check-in + digital pass for events without one, and integrates with existing badge printers rather than building our own
  • Two-mode data — in the regulated niche nothing leaves the phone (pure serverless); in the general market attendees can opt in to export their own connections/leads to a CRM or the host app
  • Genuinely creative games — a game-design craft, not a bolted-on feature: Intent Match, Spy, Booth Crawl, Battleship, Minesweeper/team missions, custom-branded per event
  • Sponsor Booth Crawl — gamified stand-visit trail driving measurable exhibitor traffic (privacy-preserving: aggregate visit counts, not attendee lead data)
  • Foreground-only + geofenced — runs only while the app is open and only inside the event boundary (checked on-device) — a built-in consent and safety guarantee
  • Organiser engagement dashboard — a separate, opt-in, aggregate-only view (connections made, missions completed, booth visits); off by default, never shows attendee identities or content

Trends

Several currents favour the concept. Data sovereignty is rising as a procurement criterion in Canadian public-sector, health and finance buying — directly favouring a serverless architecture. In-person events have rebounded and organisers are pressured to justify them with better attendee experience. On hardware, ultra-wideband is proliferating (~40% of phones shipping in 2026 carry it), so the precision-arrow moment reaches more of the room each year. The honest headwinds: the category is mature and saturated with well-funded incumbents, event demand is seasonal (answered by the subscription base), and the concept sits as an unbundled feature inside a category incumbents already occupy — the core reason the plan targets a niche where architecture, not features, is the deciding factor.

4Competition & Moat

This is a mature, saturated market, not an emerging one — an honest plan must start there. Whova (launched 2015) alone has powered 50,000+ events across 100+ countries; the large majority of organisers use an event app and ~61% of attendees adopt one. The incumbents own the attendee home screen and are well-funded and trusted. What they genuinely do better than us: (1) all-in-one platforms — Whova, Cvent and Bizzabo bundle registration, agenda, check-in, badge printing, exhibitor booths and analytics in one system, where we are a focused product; and (2) trust, scale, deep CRM integrations and badge hardware. We do matchmaking and leads too — privately, in the room, and consent-based (see positioning) — which is a different kind, not a weaker copy. The one thing we deliberately do not do is sell the scanned-badge lead-data business — in our target niche that data is a liability, not an asset.

Positioning

We don't try to be an all-in-one platform, and we don't sell the scanned-badge lead-data business — those are the incumbents' turf. But matchmaking and leads are ours too, done in a fundamentally different way, and that difference is the whole proposition: (1) Matchmaking, in the room and private — their AI matchmaking is a server-side recommender that hands you a list ('overwhelmed, not guided'); ours is real-time and proximity-based — set your intent (a role, a target company), and when a mutual match is nearby we ping you both and point an arrow, with nothing leaving the phone. (2) Leads, warm and consented — not a cold scanned badge but a mutually agreed, in-person introduction to exactly the company or role you asked for. (3) The death of the badge — we replace the laminate (widely worn, rarely read) with the thing it was always meant to do: know who's worth meeting, that they want to, and how to open. (4) Data-sovereign by architecture — the very data engine behind their lead-retrieval business disqualifies them from Canadian gov/health/finance events that mandate residency; serverless P2P is the answer they can't copy without dismantling their model. (5) Genuinely creative games — a game-design craft, custom-branded per event, where a small creative team out-executes a big platform. And rather than fight the all-in-one battle, we get in the flow by embedding into existing event apps (SDK) or offering a lightweight software check-in — not by rebuilding registration or badge hardware.

Defensibility / moat

Defensibility is the central question, not a footnote — the board scored it 3 ('feature, not a company'), and this plan does not argue that away. The proximity mechanic is copyable. What is harder to copy is the combination: a serverless architecture an incumbent can't adopt without killing its own analytics revenue, aimed at a niche (data-sovereign regulated events) where that architecture is a requirement, delivered with game-design craft. The assets that compound and are slow to copy: (1) regulated-sector reference customers + security/data-residency credentials (hard-won, and a procurement moat); (2) the creative game IP and catalogue; and (3) an embeddable-SDK path — embed the engine into incumbents rather than only compete. Honest read: this is a focused wedge, not a broad platform — sized and staged accordingly, and the plan is built to be killed cheaply at POC if the wedge doesn't hold.

9Financial Plan

The model is driven by three organiser-paid streams — a per-event licence (~$1,500), an annual subscription (~$6,000) and a sponsor Booth-Crawl (~$2,500) — against a deliberately lean cost base. Two features define the economics. First, serverless: no per-attendee hosting, so gross margin runs ~95%, and the honest trade-off (forgoing exhibitor lead-retrieval data) is a requirement, not a loss, in the target niche. Second, the build is sweat equity — the founders build iOS + Android in-house, so the app is an in-kind investment, not a cash cost, and total capital across the plan is only ~$430k. Prices are anchored to sourced competitor figures and flagged low confidence — the elasticity bet the Calgary POC validates.

The shape is a software J-curve, but a shallow one: the plan invests through the Calgary POC and scale-up, turning EBITDA-positive in Year 3 and reaching a ~48% EBITDA margin by Year 5. Financing is two small staged, gated raises. Raise 1 (~$280k: $250k equity + a $30k three-year interest-only term loan) funds a modest founder runway and the Calgary POC. Raise 2 (~$150k growth equity, Year 2) funds the Canada-wide scale-up — but only if the POC proves attendee opt-in and lands a paying regulated-sector reference. Because Raise 2 is a financing inflow, it lifts liquidity and the equity base without changing project NPV or IRR. The loan is interest-only across the build years and amortises from stabilisation (Year 4), where debt-service coverage is far above the 1.25× bank floor.

On the base case the bankability tests are met: NPV is positive, DSCR clears 1.25× comfortably from stabilisation, the balance sheet ties every year, and cash is positive at every year-end (the Year-3 point is tightest, covered by the staged raises). Three honest caveats sit alongside the results — read them as the heart of the plan, not the fine print. First, defensibility is the real question: this is a focused wedge, not a broad platform, and the moat rests on a niche (data-sovereign events) plus creative-game craft plus references that are slow to copy — the plan is built to be killed cheaply at POC if the wedge doesn't hold. Second, the headline return figures flatter: most of the NPV is terminal (exit) value, and the IRR (which computes very high) is inflated by the tiny cash outlay — because the build is free, a modest exit looks like an enormous return on the small cash in; treat this as an early-stage venture bet, not an exit-independent cash return. Third, the downside is a real loss — a bear that shocks attendee adoption (−40%), price (−20%), go-to-market cost (+30%) and product cost (+20%) together gives a negative NPV of about −$0.4M, with operating value excluding the terminal around −$0.5M and cash falling to about −$0.4M if the rollout continued. But it would not: because the growth raise is gated, the real-world downside is a choice to stop at Calgary-POC scale, containing the loss to the ~$280k first raise. The whole plan rests on two things the cheap POC is built to test before any scale capital moves: will a room actually play, and does the price hold.

Recommended path

Attack the data-sovereign Canadian regulated-sector niche first — the one place a serverless architecture is a procurement requirement and the US incumbents cannot follow — differentiate on guided-not-listed proximity and genuinely creative games, and build it in-house so the capital at risk is tiny. Prove it in a cheap Calgary POC before the gated growth raise. Keep the general-conference expansion, the embeddable SDK and the festival edition as sequenced options, not launch commitments — and be willing to stop at POC if the wedge doesn't hold.

12Implementation Roadmap

  1. Month 0–4 — Founders build the iOS + Android BLE Conference edition (private matching + the creative game modes + P2P chat + opt-in dashboard); security/data-residency audit; incorporate; line up Calgary venues and a regulated-sector pilot gate: The app works in a real venue at density, and the security audit passes, before spending on the POC
  2. Month 4–9 — Run the paid Calgary POC led into the regulated sector; instrument attendee opt-in %, connections and booth visits; pursue a paying reference customer
  3. Month 9–12 — Convert POC results into the Raise-2 decision gate: Attendee opt-in clears the density threshold AND a regulated-sector reference customer signs → trigger the ~$150k gated growth raise; if they miss, hold at POC scale (capital never committed)
  4. Year 2 — Scale across Canada; add the annual subscription and productise the sponsor Booth-Crawl; add the customer-success hire
  5. Year 3 — Add the UWB precision arrow + Wi-Fi Aware rich media; ship the embeddable SDK
  6. Year 4 — Stabilisation — EBITDA-positive, DSCR far above 1.25×; the loan begins amortising gate: Stabilised DSCR ≥ 1.25× (model: ~16.5× in Year 4) and positive operating cash flow
  7. Year 5 — Deepen the creative game catalogue and regulated references; expand cross-platform UWB; evaluate the Social/festival ('Spark') edition as act-two
