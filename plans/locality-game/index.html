1Executive Summary

Event interaction is broken. Across a real industry survey only 15% of organisers rated their event's networking 'very effective', yet networking is the reason most people attend. Canada hosts 868+ business conferences a year plus thousands of corporate events, offsites and trade shows, and the organisers behind them buy technology to fix exactly this problem — a market where trade-show and event services alone run ~CA$2.9B.

Locality Game is one proximity engine that powers a live-event game, licensed and branded by the organiser. Every attendee's phone quietly finds the right other people nearby, prompts a real-world interaction, and — on a capable phone — draws a precise on-screen radar arrow that guides them together. It is built on three deliberate constraints that are the product, not limitations: it runs peer-to-peer with no server between attendees, only while the app is open, and only inside the event's geofence. That makes a privacy promise no incumbent can honestly make — nothing is tracked, and nothing about an attendee ever leaves their phone — which is precisely what lifts opt-in above the adoption threshold a proximity game needs to be fun.

The launch product is the Conference edition ('Play'): configurable games — goal-matched networking, a spy/target hunt, and a sponsor-funded Booth Crawl that drives exhibitor traffic — that get professional attendees circulating and meeting the right people. It is sold to the organiser (never the attendee), Canada-first, through three revenue streams: a per-event licence, an annual subscription for repeat buyers (venues, conference series, agencies, corporate learning teams), and the sponsor Booth-Crawl add-on. The festival/concert 'Spark' edition is the same engine and the roadmap's second act — described here but deliberately kept out of the financials until its market is proven.

The economics are unusual and strong: because the game is serverless, there is no per-attendee cloud hosting — the cost every rival event app carries — so gross margin runs near ~95%. The plan invests through three years of losses to build that engine and prove adoption in Canadian pilots, turning EBITDA-positive in Year 4 and reaching a ~42% EBITDA margin by Year 5. Financing mirrors the staging: ~$550k now for the MVP and pilots, and a ~$300k growth round in Year 3 gated on the pilots. On the base case the project clears every bankability test — positive NPV, an internal rate of return well above the cost of capital, and debt-service coverage far above the 1.25× floor from stabilisation (Year 4), with the balance sheet tying out every year and cash positive throughout.

Two caveats are stated plainly. First, almost all of the modelled value is terminal (exit) value — the explicit five-year operating cash flows are roughly present-value-neutral — so this should be read as an early-stage venture whose return depends on building a saleable asset, not as an exit-independent cash return. Second, the honest downside is real: a stress case that shocks attendee adoption, price, go-to-market cost and product cost together is genuinely loss-making. But because the growth raise is gated, that downside is a choice to stop at pilot scale — containing the loss to the ~$550k first raise — rather than a national commitment that fails. That is exactly what the cheap Canadian pilots are designed to test before the larger capital is deployed.

2The Product / Service

Locality Game is one proximity engine that powers two editions of a live-event game, licensed and branded by the event organiser. Every attendee's phone quietly finds the right other people nearby, prompts a real-world interaction, and — on capable phones — draws a precise on-screen "radar arrow" that guides them together. It is built on three deliberate constraints that are the product, not limitations: it runs peer-to-peer with no server between attendees, it works only while the app is open (so a closed app is provably not scanning), and it works only inside the event's geofence (so it can never be repurposed to find people out in the world). The Conference edition ("Play") — the launch product — turns a work conference into a game that gets people to circulate, meet the right contacts and visit the exhibitor stands. The Social edition ("Spark") — the roadmap's second act — brings the same engine to festivals and concerts as an opt-in way to meet people in the crowd.

Value proposition

For the organiser (the buyer): event interaction is broken — in one industry survey only 15% of organisers rated their event's networking 'very effective' — and Locality Game turns the dead networking hour into a game people actually play, steering them to the right people and the paying booths. Because nothing is tracked and nothing leaves the attendee's phone, it earns the trust that lifts opt-in above the ~30-40% adoption ceiling that makes most event features fall flat — and a proximity game is only fun once enough of the room is in. And it offers a moment no incumbent can: on a capable phone, an ultra-precise arrow that literally points you to the person you're meant to meet. For the attendee: find the people worth meeting — matched privately to your goals — get a fun reason to walk up to a stranger, and be guided right to them. No feed, no data harvesting, no strangers from the internet: only people who are here, now, and have chosen to play.

Key features

  • One engine, two editions — a Conference game ("Play") and a Social/festival game ("Spark") on the same proximity platform; the organiser brands and configures it
  • Serverless peer-to-peer at the attendee layer — matches, profiles and chats never leave the two phones; there is no server that could see, store or sell them
  • Private matching (PSI) — you set what you're looking for and are matched only when it's mutual; nobody, not even a listener nearby, learns your criteria unless there's a match
  • A three-layer radar — detect someone's nearby, confirm a match, then (on capable phones) a precise distance-and-direction arrow that guides you to them
  • Configurable game modes — Icebreaker Match, Spy, Booth Crawl, Battleship, Minesweeper/team missions — switched on per event
  • Sponsor Booth Crawl — a gamified stand-visit trail that drives measurable exhibitor traffic, funded from sponsor budget
  • Foreground-only + geofenced — the game runs only while the app is open and only inside the event boundary (checked on-device), a built-in consent and safety guarantee
  • Organiser engagement dashboard — a separate, opt-in, aggregate-only view of connections made, missions completed and booth visits; off by default, and never shows attendee identities or content
  • Graceful hardware tiering — a full warmer/colder game on every phone (the universal floor), and the precise arrow as a premium moment on capable devices — core play never depends on the arrow

Customer journey

  • Organiser creates the event, draws the geofence, picks the game modes and the goal/interest taxonomy, and brands the app
  • Attendees join the event (via the existing event app, a QR code or a link) and set a profile plus what they're looking for — with clear, foreground consent

  • The organiser watches live engagement (if they opted into the dashboard) and renews per-event or moves to an annual plan

The Canadian scale-up (gated on the pilots)

The plan is staged and gated. Year 1 proves the Conference edition in a handful of paid Canadian conference pilots on iOS, measuring the two swing variables — attendee opt-in % (does enough of the room play?) and price (does the per-event licence hold?). Only if those clear does the ~$300k growth raise deploy in Year 3, funding the cross-platform build (UWB arrow + Android) and the sales/customer-success scale-up. From there the base case widens across the Canadian conference, trade-show and corporate-event pool, adding the annual-subscription base (series, agencies, corporate learning) that turns one-off events into recurring revenue, and reaching ~$1.97M by Year 5. Because the large commitment is gated, the downside is contained to the ~$550k pilot raise — a choice to stop, not a national failure.

Trends

Several currents favour the concept. In-person events have rebounded and organisers are under pressure to justify them with better attendee experience. Privacy is becoming a buying criterion — a serverless, nothing-leaves-the-phone game is a genuine differentiator as attendees tire of data-harvesting apps. On hardware, ultra-wideband is proliferating — roughly 40% of phones shipping in 2026 carry UWB — so the precision-arrow 'magic moment' reaches more of the room each year, and cross-platform interop is a live tailwind (Apple's iOS 26 Wi-Fi Aware framework and 2026 iPhone↔Android UWB ranging, pushed along by EU interoperability rules). The headwinds are honest: event demand is seasonal (answered by the subscription base), and the concept sits as an unbundled feature inside a category incumbents already occupy.

4Competition & Moat

Event gamification and networking are already served — but as bundled features of all-in-one event platforms or as non-proximity tools. Whova ships a native 'Icebreaker Contest'; Goosechase runs event scavenger hunts; Brella, Swapcard and Grip sell AI matchmaking and networking. None offers a real-time, proximity, peer-to-peer game that matches people to the right people and physically guides them together, and none can make the serverless privacy claim.

Positioning

Not another matchmaking tab inside an event app — a proximity game that produces the outcome organisers actually want (people meeting the right people, and traffic to the booths), with a privacy stance (serverless, geofenced, foreground-only) none of the suite vendors can claim and a precision 'arrow' moment none of them can build. Competes on gameplay depth, precision and privacy, not on out-featuring a full event suite.

Defensibility / moat

Honestly assessed, defensibility is the weak point — this reads as a feature an incumbent could bundle (Whova's existing icebreaker contest shows the intent), which is why the board scored the moat low. The plan does not pretend otherwise. The durable edges it builds toward are four: (1) genuine gameplay depth (a game studio's craft, not a checkbox feature); (2) the serverless-privacy stance as brand and trust, which is architecturally hard for a data-driven incumbent to copy without rebuilding; (3) the UWB precision experience, which is real engineering few will match early; and (4) an embeddable SDK — the strategic hedge of embedding into the incumbent platforms (and their installed base) rather than only competing with them.

All three of Locality's prices are flagged low confidence — price is the strongest lever on the model and an elasticity bet the pilots validate before the growth raise.

Unit economics

The defining fact is serverless economics: with no per-attendee cloud hosting, the marginal cost to serve an event is essentially payment fees plus a thin optional analytics backend, so gross margin runs ~95% — above the listed event-software comparable (Eventbrite ~67–70% gross) precisely because of the architecture. Customer success, onboarding and support are real costs but scale with the team as operating expenses, not cost-of-goods — so they sit in operating margin, and the business turns strongly cash-generative once the fixed team is covered (EBITDA-positive in Year 4, ~42% margin by Year 5).

Channels

B2B and reference-led, not consumer virality: land a paid pilot → prove the room plays (opt-in above the density threshold) and the price holds → expand the organiser to more events or an annual subscription → attach the sponsor Booth-Crawl at trade shows. The pilots are the product's proof and the sales engine's fuel; the gated growth raise scales the motion only once they clear.

Customer acquisition

Acquisition is a B2B sales cost, not consumer paid-acquisition. Marketing programmes (industry events, business-development travel, content) are modelled top-down at ~20% of revenue in Year 1 declining to ~12% by Year 5 as brand and referrals compound; sales and customer-success salaries are separate, in the team cost base, so the two are not double-counted. The load-bearing commercial unknown is not cost-per-click but attendee opt-in % — whether enough of a given room plays for the game to work — which the pilots measure directly.

Launch plan

Land the iOS Conference edition at a handful of friendly Canadian conferences; instrument attendee opt-in, connections made and booth visits; convert those results into the reference cases and the evidence for the gated growth raise.

7Operations

The product is a mobile app the organiser configures and brands. The attendee-to-attendee game is serverless peer-to-peer — no central infrastructure carries attendee data, matches or chats — so operations are light: onboarding and success for organisers, and a thin, opt-in analytics backend for the (optional) engagement dashboard. The geofence and foreground-only behaviour are enforced on-device.

Supply chain

The 'supply chain' is engineering and platform: native iOS (then Android) apps integrating low-level radios (BLE, then UWB and Wi-Fi Aware), the PSI cryptographic match handshake, and the opt-in telemetry backend. Cross-platform depth (UWB arrow, rich-media chat) is deferred to the gated Phase 2, tracking the maturing device ecosystem.

Location & facilities

Lean and largely remote — a software team, no retail or venue premises. Costs are people and tooling, not facilities.

Staffing plan

Lean at launch — the founding team covers product, engineering and finance in-house, building the MVP (a large share of the build is sweat equity). A sales/partnerships hire is added mid-Year 1 and a customer-success/event-operations hire in Year 2 as event volume grows, with engineering, sales and success scaling through the plan as revenue supports them.

9Financial Plan

The model is driven by three organiser-paid streams — a per-event licence (~$1,500), an annual subscription (~$6,000) and a sponsor Booth-Crawl add-on (~$2,500) — against a lean, scaling cost base whose defining feature is serverless economics: with no per-attendee hosting, gross margin runs ~95%. All prices are anchored to sourced competitor list prices and flagged low confidence — price is the strongest lever and the elasticity bet the pilots validate. Both prices and unit costs escalate 3%/yr, so margin does not drift.

The shape is a classic software J-curve: the plan invests through three years of losses to build the engine and prove adoption, turning EBITDA-positive in Year 4 and reaching a ~42% EBITDA margin by Year 5. Financing is two staged, gated raises. Raise 1 (~$550k: $500k equity + a $50k three-year interest-only term loan) funds the MVP and the Canadian pilots through the ramp, holding a cash cushion. Raise 2 (~$300k growth equity, taken in Year 3) funds the cross-platform build and the scale-up — but only if the pilots prove attendee opt-in and price. Because Raise 2 is a financing inflow, it lifts liquidity and the equity base without changing the project's NPV or IRR. The loan is interest-only across the build years and amortises from stabilisation (Year 4), where debt-service coverage is far above the 1.25× bank floor.

On the base case the bankability tests are met: net present value is positive, the internal rate of return is well above the cost of capital, DSCR clears 1.25× comfortably from stabilisation, the balance sheet ties out every year, and cash is positive at every year-end (the Year-2 trough is the tightest point, covered by Raise 1). Two honest caveats sit alongside those results. First — and this is the most important thing a reader should take from the numbers — almost all of the modelled NPV is terminal (exit) value; the explicit five-year operating cash flows are roughly present-value-neutral. This is an early-stage venture whose modelled return rests on building a saleable asset at the stated (conservative, 6× EBITDA) exit multiple and on the discount rate, both flagged placeholders — it is not an exit-independent cash return, and the page's §9 shows the ex-terminal figure directly. Second, the downside is genuinely loss-making: a bear that shocks attendee adoption (−40% on volume), price (−20%), go-to-market cost (+30%) and product cost (+20%) together drives operating losses across the plan and a negative NPV of about −$0.9M — there is no terminal-value flattery hiding it (in the bear even the terminal value is negative, because Year-5 EBITDA is negative). The bear's operating value excluding the terminal is roughly −$0.7M, and its cash would fall to about −$0.46M by Year 5 if the rollout continued. But it would not: because the growth raise is gated on the pilots, the real-world downside is a choice to stop at pilot scale — containing the loss to the ~$550k first raise — not a national commitment that fails. The whole plan therefore leans on two things the cheap Canadian pilots are built to test before the larger capital moves: whether enough of the room opts in, and whether the price holds.

Recommended path

Launch the Conference edition on iOS, sold to Canadian organisers, and prove attendee opt-in and price in cheap paid pilots before deploying the gated growth raise on the cross-platform build and scale-up. Keep the festival ('Spark') edition and the embeddable SDK as sequenced options, not launch commitments — the discipline that keeps the first raise small and the downside a choice to stop.

12Implementation Roadmap

  1. Month 0–4 — Build the lean iOS Conference MVP (BLE detect + PSI match + core game modes + P2P chat + opt-in dashboard); incorporate; line up friendly Canadian conference pilots gate: MVP demonstrably works in a real venue at density before spending on pilots
  2. Month 4–8 — Run paid Canadian conference pilots; instrument attendee opt-in %, connections made and booth visits
  3. Month 8–12 — Convert pilot results into reference cases and the Raise-2 decision gate: Attendee opt-in clears the density threshold and the per-event price holds → trigger the ~$300k gated growth raise; if they miss, hold at pilot scale (the capital is never committed)
  4. Year 2 — Add the annual subscription tier and productise the sponsor Booth-Crawl; grow the Canadian event base; add the customer-success hire
  5. Year 3 — Build the UWB precision arrow + Android (funded by the growth raise); scale sales and customer success
  6. Year 4 — Stabilisation — EBITDA-positive, DSCR far above 1.25×; the loan begins amortising gate: Stabilised DSCR ≥ 1.25× (model: ~7.6× in Year 4, rising to ~22.4× in Year 5) and positive operating cash flow
  7. Year 5 — Ship the embeddable SDK (embed into incumbent platforms) and launch the Social/festival ('Spark') edition as act-two; expand cross-platform as the device ecosystem matures
