1Executive Summary

Canada has no homegrown leader in premium, authentic Asian pantry flavours, and the mainstream specialty channel is thin on the real thing. Asian Flavours fills that gap with a branded house of authentic pastes, oils and seasonings organised into country Kitchens (Japan, China, Thailand), each paired with traditional recipes a home cook makes from ordinary Canadian groceries plus our flavour.

The category is real and growing — premium Asian condiments have been proven by a wave of funded brands — but most competitors sell a single hero condiment. Asian Flavours instead sells a recipe-led flavour system, and it sells it entirely direct-to-consumer (DTC) — the highest-margin route, where the brand keeps the full retail price rather than handing a cut to retailers or distributors. Revenue comes two ways: a recurring Kitchen Club subscription box, and one-off refills of individual flavours (the fast-movers run out well before the slow ones).

The range is priced $1.50 below the ~$20 premium chili-crisp leader — genuinely premium, yet visibly the cheaper authentic option — and that pricing is what makes the economics work on a modest raise. Execution is staged to de-risk it: a single-Kitchen proof-of-concept (the six SKUs of the China line, launched in Calgary, whose Chinese community is ~12× the next-largest Asian diaspora) proves repeat, retention and — critically — that the premium price holds its volume, before the rollout widens to all three Kitchens in Calgary (Year 2), across western Canada (Year 3), nationally (Year 4), and a five-Kitchen national range (Year 5) — reaching the order of $4.1M in revenue by Year 5.

The financing mirrors that staging: two gated raises — ~$175k now for the Calgary proof-of-concept, and a ~$300k growth round in Year 3 taken only if the PoC proves out. Years 1–2 are an equity-funded ramp; from stabilisation (Year 3) the business is strongly cash-generative, covering its debt service many times over. The project returns are strong — a strongly positive net present value and a high internal rate of return — though a material share of the headline value (~three-quarters) rests on a terminal exit multiple, so on operating cash flows alone the five-year return is far more modest. The downside is treated honestly: at this premium a stress case combining weak demand, higher acquisition cost and higher product cost is roughly break-even rather than loss-making — but with a thin margin of safety that rests on the premium price holding its volume. That is exactly what the low-cost Calgary proof-of-concept tests before the national commitment is made.

2The Product / Service

Asian Flavours is a branded house of authentic Asian pantry flavours, organised into country Kitchens. Each Kitchen is a curated set of signature flavour products — pastes, oils, fermented condiments and seasoning blends — paired with traditional recipes the customer cooks using ordinary Canadian grocery ingredients plus our flavour. The full vision is three Kitchens — Japan, China and Thailand (16 SKUs, ~17 recipes) — but launch is a lean single-Kitchen proof-of-concept: the six-SKU China line, in Calgary (chosen because Calgary's Chinese community is by far the largest Asian diaspora, and the immigrant segment buys only its origin Kitchen). The commercial insight is that our jars bundle the exact authentic components a Canadian can't easily buy fresh — lemongrass, galangal and kaffir lime in a Thai curry paste; kombu-and-bonito in a dashi pack; aged fermented pastes in the Chinese line — so every recipe stays fully shoppable at a mainstream supermarket. The promise: our flavour + your everyday groceries = the real thing.

Value proposition

For the home cook: genuinely authentic Japanese, Chinese and Thai cooking at home, with no specialty-store hunt and a recipe that guarantees the result — solving the 'I bought the jar, now what?' problem that kills repeat purchase in the category. The Kitchen Club subscription box turns that into a habit — a recurring delivery of flavours + recipes, so there's always something new to cook — while one-off refills let customers restock the individual flavours they burn through fastest. Both are sold direct-to-consumer, the highest-margin route, with a recipe-led content engine turning discovery into purchase and driving repeat.

Key features

  • Three country Kitchens (Japan, China, Thailand) — a coherent, collectible flavour system rather than a scatter of SKUs
  • Signature flavour products spanning the authentic flavour axes of each cuisine (umami/fermented, spicy/mala, sour, aromatic, nutty)
  • Traditional recipes, each heroing our flavours with every other ingredient available at a mainstream Canadian grocer
  • Flavours bundle the hard-to-source authentic components, so the shopper never has to find fresh galangal or real dashi
  • A recurring Kitchen Club subscription box — the acquisition and retention engine — plus one-off refills of individual flavours as they deplete at different rates
  • A sourced-authentic core — protected-origin staples like Pixian doubanjiang and Chinkiang black vinegar — as the differentiator and supply moat
  • Authentic origin-country branding — design and packaging rooted in each cuisine's own visual language (sourced from the origin countries)
  • Direct-to-consumer only — the highest-margin channel; no retailer, grocery or distributor cut
  • Shelf-stable range (jars, pouches, shakers, filter-packs) — low perishability, import- and co-pack-friendly, cheap to ship

Customer journey

  • Discover a dish through the recipe content engine (site, video, social)
  • Join the Kitchen Club (recurring box) or buy an individual flavour to start
  • Cook the traditional recipe using everyday groceries + our flavour — and it actually tastes right
  • Re-order the fast-moving flavours as one-off refills between boxes
  • Stay subscribed for new Kitchens and recipes; collect across Japan → China → Thailand

The national opportunity (gated on the PoC)

Rollout is staged by geography and gated on the PoC: Y1 proves the China Kitchen in Calgary; Y2 opens all three Kitchens across Calgary; Y3 takes the three-Kitchen range across western Canada (Vancouver 475k Chinese-origin, Edmonton, Winnipeg); Y4 goes national (Toronto 669k Chinese, Montréal, Ottawa); Y5 adds two further Kitchens for a five-Kitchen national range. The qualified market grows with each step — ~45k in Calgary, ~273k across the west, ~678k nationally — and the plan captures ~3% of it by Year 5, reaching ~19,500 customers / ~$4.1M. Premium pricing keeps the capital modest and staged — a ~$175k proof-of-concept raise now, then a ~$300k national growth round gated on the PoC (Year 3) — rather than the far larger single raise a value-priced version would need. The economics still swing on acquisition cost, which the Calgary PoC validates cheaply before the national commitment is made.

Search-demand cross-check (preliminary)

A second, independent check uses Google Keyword Planner (Canada), and it validates the strategy directly. Buying-intent flavour terms draw ~86,000 searches/month nationally (Calgary ~3,786), with chili crisp the product hero at ~50,000/month, +900% year-on-year. The bigger signal is in discovery: the three signature dishes — kung pao chicken, mapo tofu and dan dan noodles — each draw ~50,000 searches/month (~150,000 combined) — while generic category terms ('asian condiments') are near-dead (~50/month). People search dishes and specific products, not 'condiments', which proves the recipe-to-commerce thesis. On conversion — the honest mechanics: capturing ~1% of those searches as a jar sale (0.5% as a bundle) is a mature, organic-led figure across all channels, not a paid-ad conversion rate. Paid ads alone convert only about 0.05–0.1% of searches into sales (roughly ~5% click through, and a new premium direct-to-consumer food brand converts a conservative ~1% of clicks), so paid is a small slice; the ~150,000/month organic dish-search pool captured through the recipe content engine (SEO), not ad spend, is the affordable route to that ~1% capture. On that basis the flavour terms imply ~$335k/year nationally / ~$15k Calgary at mature capture — a floor the brand ramps toward, and a number the Calgary PoC is designed to test. Search also under-counts the 1st-gen Chinese diaspora (who buy via community and Chinese-language channels, which the census sizing captures).

Trends

Experiential home cooking, the mainstreaming of authentic regional Asian cuisine, premium-condiment growth, subscription commerce, and recipe-led social selling all favour the concept. The headwind is a crowded chili-crisp segment and the working-capital demands of importing.

4Competition & Moat

The premium Asian-condiment space has funded, visible players — but almost all sell a single hero condiment, most heavily in chili crisp, as one-off purchases. No brand owns a coherent, multi-cuisine 'Kitchen + recipe' system with a recurring subscription in Canada, and no homegrown Canadian leader exists.

Premium chili-crisp brands (e.g. Fly By Jing, Momofuku)Strong single-hero brands; proof the premium price holds, but narrow assortment and no subscription/recipe layer
Value incumbents (e.g. Lao Gan Ma)Cheap and ubiquitous — the value anchor, not the premium/authentic position
Curry-paste & pantry importers (e.g. Aroy-D, Mae Ploy)Authentic and cheap, but commodity brand with no recipe/content or direct relationship

Positioning

Not another single-hero condiment — a curated, collectible country-Kitchen system with a recurring subscription, where recipes drive purchase and repeat. Competing on completeness, authenticity, the recipe-to-commerce experience and a direct customer relationship, rather than out-shouting the chili-crisp incumbents on their own turf.

Defensibility / moat

Defensibility comes from three layers, none of them the commoditised jar itself: (1) a recipe-led content engine + subscription that lowers acquisition cost, builds an owned audience and locks in recurring revenue and retention; (2) exclusive/preferential supply of protected-origin staples (Pixian doubanjiang, Chinkiang black vinegar) — the only genuinely durable supply moat, and the #1 thing to lock; (3) the direct customer relationship and data (who cooks what, what they reorder), which a wholesale brand never owns. Building toward a recognised multi-cuisine subscription brand is the durable asset.

5Business Model

Pricing

Premium tier — a subscription box at ~$54 (three flavours + recipes, ~$18/jar) and refill jars at ~$18.50 — priced $1.50 below the ~$20 premium chili-crisp leader (Fly By Jing, Momofuku), while sitting far above the value incumbents (Lao Gan Ma ~$5). The refill jar carries the flagship price because refills are dominated by the fast-moving chili crisp; the box blends in the lower-anchored SKUs, so it sits at the top of its defensible band — a deliberate, visible 'authentic but cheaper than the leader' position the PoC validates. Everything is sold direct at full retail — no retailer or distributor cut — which is what makes DTC the high-margin channel.

Unit economics

Gross margin is very high (~75%+) at the premium price with no channel cut. Per jar, DTC keeps on the order of $15 (premium retail less payment fees and shipping); a wholesale route would surrender the direct customer relationship and data and a share of that margin to a distributor. The recurring subscription lifts lifetime value and smooths cash, while refills add high-margin reorders; the cost that matters is customer acquisition (~$50 blended, modelled from real search cost-per-click), which the content engine and subscription retention keep efficient.

6Go-to-Market

Channels

  • A dish-led recipe-to-commerce site + owned content: the three signature dishes (kung pao chicken, mapo tofu, dan dan noodles) each draw ~50,000 Canadian searches/month — a ~150,000/month organic funnel to capture with recipes
  • The Kitchen Club subscription as the primary conversion + retention mechanism
  • One-off refill reorders driven by email/app prompts as flavours run low
  • Competitor-term capture — ~5,000 Canadians/month search 'Fly By Jing', 'Lao Gan Ma' and 'Momofuku Chili Crunch' by name; capture that intent with comparison content ('the authentic alternative to…') and targeted competitor-term ads

Fully self-serve and content-led: discover a recipe → buy a flavour or join the Kitchen Club → cook → reorder. No field sales. The proof-of-concept runs this at small scale to measure real repeat, retention, basket size and acquisition cost before scaling spend.

Customer acquisition

Marketing is modelled bottom-up from customer-acquisition cost, not a flat percentage. Google's own forecast puts the search cost-per-click for these terms at only ~$1.21 (mobile as low as $0.27) — low for e-commerce — so blended with the organic recipe engine the model uses ~$50 to acquire each new customer, against ~$140/yr of revenue and a multi-year subscription lifetime — a strong payback. In the model this acquisition cost falls from ~41% of revenue in the launch year to ~22% by Year 5 as the retained subscriber base compounds (classic subscription economics). CAC remains the key sensitivity — competitive head terms cost more and real click-to-buy conversion is unproven — which is exactly what the proof-of-concept measures, and the bear case now genuinely stress-tests a higher-CAC world (marketing/CAC +30% on top of weak demand and higher product cost).

Launch plan

Land the China Kitchen's six SKUs on the DTC store in Calgary, with the recipe engine and the Kitchen Club live; capture subscriber retention, refill reorder rates, AOV and CAC; convert those results into the case for the national scale-up and its growth raise.

7Operations

Shelf-stable products are imported and/or co-packed from Asia and fulfilled direct to consumers — recurring subscription boxes on a schedule plus one-off refill orders. The range is deliberately ambient (jars, pouches, shakers, filter-packs) to avoid cold-chain and perishability and to keep shipping cheap.

Supply chain

A mix of own-produced/co-packed shelf-stable core (chili crisp, curry pastes, chili jam, tamarind, shakers, sesame pastes, miso) and sourced-authentic protected-origin staples (Pixian doubanjiang, Chinkiang black vinegar) imported genuine. Supplier diversification is needed to avoid single-source risk; locking preferential supply on the protected-origin heroes is the strategic priority.

Location & facilities

A modest storage/fulfilment footprint plus light 3PL for pick-pack-ship of boxes and refills; no retail premises. Founders self-fulfil during the proof-of-concept, adding a fulfilment hire as subscriber volume grows.

Staffing plan

Lean at launch — founders cover sourcing, brand/content and finance; a fulfilment/operations lead is added toward the end of Year 1 as box volume grows. Regulatory: a Safe Food for Canadians import licence and Preventive Control Plan; confirm CFIA specifics per product (e.g. Sichuan peppercorn import status).

9Financial Plan

The model is driven by two direct-to-consumer lines — a recurring Kitchen Club subscription box (~$54) and one-off jar refills (~$18.50) — against a lean, scaling cost base. Both are priced $1.50 below the ~$20 premium chili-crisp leader, which is the single most important driver of returns: at that premium, gross margin is ~75%+ with no channel cut. All assumptions are flagged for confidence; the load-bearing ones are whether the premium price holds its volume (elasticity), customer-acquisition cost, and co-pack product cost — the three things the proof-of-concept validates. Marketing is built bottom-up from a real ~$50 blended acquisition cost per new customer (grounded in Google's own search cost-per-click), declining as a share of revenue as the subscriber base compounds.

The plan is financed in two staged, gated raises. Raise 1 (~$175k: $145k equity + a $30k two-year interest-only loan) funds the Calgary proof-of-concept through all three Kitchens in Calgary, holding a comfortable cash reserve. Raise 2 (~$300k growth equity, taken in Year 3) funds the national rollout — but only if the PoC proves out. Because Raise 2 is a financing inflow, it lifts liquidity and the equity base without changing the project's net present value or return. Years 1–2 are an equity-funded ramp (the loan is interest-only over exactly those years); from stabilisation (Year 3) the business covers its debt service many times over.

The bankability tests are met comfortably: net present value is strongly positive, the internal rate of return is high, the debt-service coverage ratio sits far above the 1.25× floor from stabilisation, the balance sheet ties every year, and cash is positive at every year-end. Two honest caveats sit alongside those results. First, a material share of the headline NPV (~three-quarters) is the terminal exit-multiple value — on operating cash flows alone the return is far more modest, so this should not be read as an exit-independent return. Second, the downside, while improved by the premium, is thin: a stress case combining weak demand (−50% volume), a −20% price, +20% product cost and a +30% acquisition cost is now roughly break-even (operating value only marginally positive, about +$8k excluding the exit multiple) with cash holding at a razor-thin Year-2 trough — a marked improvement over a value-priced version (which is deeply loss-making under the same stress), but not a comfortable cushion. The bear's positive headline NPV is almost entirely the exit multiple and assumes the Year-3 growth raise is deployed — but in a genuine bear that raise is gated and would not be taken; the honest read of the downside is therefore the roughly break-even operating result and a choice to stop at Calgary scale, not a national going concern. The whole plan now leans on the premium price holding its volume, which is precisely why the national commitment (Raise 2) is gated behind the cheap Calgary PoC that tests elasticity directly. Working capital is the central operational constraint — importing means pre-paying inventory 3–4 months before sale — so the model carries a long inventory cycle; year-end liquidity holds, while the intra-year timing through the Year-2→Year-3 scale-up is a residual risk the PoC, real co-pack quotes and the Year-3 growth raise are structured to de-risk.

12Implementation Roadmap

  1. Month 0–3 — Get real co-pack MOQ + tolling quotes; finalise the China Kitchen (6 SKUs), authentic origin branding, SFC licence, first import order; build the DTC store + subscription gate: Co-pack unit economics confirm the ~$3/jar COGS before drawing capital
  2. Month 3–5 — Calgary PoC live: DTC store + recipe engine, Kitchen Club subscription open, first China-line stock landed at premium price points
  3. Month 6–9 — Measure subscription retention, refill reorder rates, AOV and CAC in Calgary gate: Retention + AOV + CAC hit target → trigger the ~$300k national growth raise (Raise 2) and widen the rollout; if they miss, the national capital is never committed
  4. Year 2 — All three Kitchens (Japan, China, Thailand) across Calgary; add the fulfilment hire; lock preferential protected-origin supply
  5. Year 3 — Three-Kitchen range across western Canada (Vancouver, Edmonton, Winnipeg + smaller western cities) gate: Stabilised DSCR ≥ 1.25× (model: ~8×) and strong operating cash flow
  6. Year 4 — Go national — add the eastern cities (Toronto, Montréal, Ottawa); lock exclusive protected-origin supply as the durable moat
  7. Year 5 — Add two further Kitchens (five-Kitchen national range); deepen the recipe library and the collectible flavour system
